## PART 3 — VALUE-BASED PRICING FRAMEWORK

### Philosophy

Value-based pricing means you charge based on the value and ROI you create for your client as a result of the project — not hours spent. The client's question is always "what does this solve for me and what is that worth?" not "how many hours did this take?"

Practically: before proposing a price, understand the cost of the problem. A client with a dead router during a busy workday is losing billable hours. A small business with no website is losing customers to a competitor who has one. A company running undocumented DNS is one engineer departure away from an outage. Those costs anchor the conversation.

### How to Price a Value-Based Package (Process)

1. **Ask the diagnostic questions** (see Part 4). Let the client describe the problem in their words.
2. **Identify the cost of the problem.** Lost time, lost revenue, customer frustration, reputational risk. Put a number on it — even a rough one.
3. **Identify the value of the solution.** What does it unlock? More sales? Fewer support calls? Being able to work from home reliably?
4. **Price at 10–20% of the one-year value of the solution.** A client who loses $500/month in productivity due to a broken home network gets a $3,000–6,000 annual return from a $299 network setup. Your $299 is 5–10% of their annual problem cost. That's a strong ROI they can see.
5. **Present 3 options.** Always. A starter, a standard, and a premium. Most clients self-select to the middle. The premium anchors the conversation and makes the standard feel reasonable.
6. **Never quote hourly to a SOHO client.** It creates anxiety about clock-watching and invites scope arguments. Use flat-rate packages. Hourly is only appropriate for ongoing retainers or open-ended enterprise work where scope can't be fixed upfront.

## PART 4 — INITIAL CLIENT CONSULTING QUESTIONNAIRE

### Purpose

This questionnaire runs every discovery conversation — whether that's a 10-minute Nextdoor DM exchange or a 30-minute Zoom call. The goal is not to interrogate the client; it's to understand the real problem underneath the stated problem, assess whether you can create meaningful value, and build trust by demonstrating that you listen before you prescribe.

Elite consultants distinguish themselves by asking strategic, purposeful questions that uncover hidden opportunities and reveal the true cost of inaction — not by rushing to showcase their knowledge.

Use these questions conversationally. Pick 5–7 relevant ones per engagement. Never read from a list.

---

### Section 1 — Discovery (Use for all clients, first contact)

1. "What's going on right now that made you reach out?" *(Open. Let them talk. Don't interrupt.)*
2. "How long has this been a problem?"
3. "What have you already tried?"
4. "Is this affecting just you, or is it impacting your team or your customers?"
5. "What does a good outcome look like — what would be different in your day-to-day?"

---

### Section 2 — Qualification (Assess fit and budget readiness)

6. "Have you worked with an IT consultant or web developer before? How did that go?"
7. "Is there a timeline driving this — a launch date, an event, a deadline?"
8. "Who else is involved in the decision to move forward?" *(If they say "I need to check with my spouse/partner/boss," note it — the decision-maker isn't in the room.)*
9. "Do you have a budget in mind, or do you need me to scope it out first?" *(Don't be afraid to ask this. It respects both parties' time.)*
10. "Are you talking to anyone else about this, or are we the first conversation?" *(Tells you how competitive the situation is.)*

---

### Section 3 — Value Discovery (Anchor the ROI conversation)

11. "What's the cost to you — in time, money, or customers — if this doesn't get fixed in the next 30 days?"
12. "If this were working perfectly, what would that enable you to do that you can't do right now?"
13. "What's most important to you in choosing someone for this: speed, price, expertise, or long-term relationship?"

---

### Section 4 — Technical Scoping (Use for IT/web engagements)

14. "Walk me through your current setup — equipment, ISP, how many devices are connected."
15. "Do you own your domain name, or does someone else manage it for you?"
16. "Are you the only person who accesses the system, or do others rely on it?"
17. "Is there anything I should know before I start — existing contracts, equipment leases, or systems I shouldn't touch?"

---

### Listening Red Flags During Discovery

These are signals to probe deeper before agreeing to take the work:

- They can't describe what success looks like.
- They already know what the solution is and just want you to execute it. *(May mean they want cheap labor, not consulting.)*
- They mention a previous consultant who "ripped them off" without context.
- The budget they mention is below your floor for the scope they're describing.
- They can't identify a decision-maker.
- They want everything done "as soon as possible" with no timeline attached.

## PART 5 — CLIENT QUALIFICATION GUIDE

### Sign Immediately

- They described the problem clearly and candidly.
- They've tried to solve it themselves and failed — they know they need help.
- The cost of the problem to them is real and they can articulate it.
- The decision-maker is in the conversation.
- They asked about your process, not just your price.
- They've worked with a consultant before and had a good experience.
- The scope matches one of your packages without significant customization.
- They're willing to pay a deposit upfront.

### Proceed with Caution

- The budget is below your floor but the client seems genuinely good — consider a starter package and set hard scope limits.
- The timeline is tight but technically feasible — add a rush fee (20–25% on top of base).
- They want hourly billing — convert them to a package by explaining the advantages of fixed-scope work for both parties.
- They mention a prior bad experience but can explain it rationally — give them the benefit of the doubt with a written scope agreement.

### Walk Away

- They want you to match a competitor's price without discussing value or scope.
- They claim to have a budget, then reveal it after you've scoped the work and it's less than half your proposal.
- They want to start without any written agreement.
- They called the previous consultant incompetent but can't explain what specifically went wrong.
- They want features or deliverables outside your defined service exclusions and won't accept the boundary.
- They're evasive about who makes the final decision.
- They've contacted 5+ providers and are purely price-shopping.
- They want to "see how the first project goes" before paying — this is unpaid spec work in disguise.
- Any client who requires you to sign an NDA before even discussing the problem (without a corresponding paid discovery fee).

*How to File:*
- Go to https://commerce.utah.gov/corp/.
- Select "File Annual Report".
